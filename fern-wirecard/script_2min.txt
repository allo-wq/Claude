It's the evening of June 19th, 2020. A private jet waits on a small airfield south of Vienna. A man in a navy suit climbs aboard, carrying almost nothing. Its destination is Minsk.

His name is Jan Marsalek. Until yesterday, he ran operations at Wirecard, a German payments company once worth more than Deutsche Bank.

Then the company admitted something almost impossible. 1.9 billion euros were missing. And they probably never existed.

Marsalek joined Wirecard at 20, a self-taught coder from Vienna. By 30, he was on the board. Colleagues called him charming, restless, and impossible to read. Officially, Wirecard processed card payments around the world. But roughly half its revenue came from three obscure partners in Dubai, Singapore, and the Philippines.

Journalists at the Financial Times kept asking questions. The numbers were too smooth. The partners were too hard to find. Wirecard dismissed every report. Germany's response? Its financial regulator investigated the reporters.

Then the auditors asked two banks in Manila for proof. The answer was brutal. The documents were fake. The accounts didn't exist.

Within a week, Wirecard collapsed. It was the first company in Germany's top stock index ever to go bankrupt. Marsalek told people he was flying to Manila to find the money.

He never went.

Instead, investigators say, he went to Moscow. And that's where the story gets stranger. In 2023, British police broke up a Bulgarian spy ring operating out of a seaside guesthouse. They'd been tracking journalists across Europe. According to prosecutors, the man giving the orders was Jan Marsalek.

Today, he's reportedly living in Russia under a new name. Some believe he was a fraudster who needed powerful friends. Others think Wirecard was never the point. It was the cover.

Either way, the money is still missing. And so is he.
